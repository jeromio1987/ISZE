## LOGIC 1 — pricing discussion AS PROXY WAR

### What holds up
- FX pass-through is a real and legitimate mechanism. JPY/EUR movement does affect ISZE's procurement cost base.
- The 163.7 JPY/EUR rate (confirmed 01 Oct 2025) is a concrete, auditable reference point.
- Precedent risk from methodology concessions is a genuine institutional concern. If ISZE grants ITUK a carve-out, other distributors will observe it.

### What is uncertain
- **ISZE's actual margin on the 2026 increase is unquantified here.** The +€298,911 / +5.3% basket impact is the customer-facing number. What happened to ISZE's own margin? If ISZE buys in JPY and sells in EUR, and EUR weakened against JPY, a pure cost pass-through should be margin-neutral. Has ISZE demonstrated that the increase is exactly margin-neutral, or is the FX logic providing cover for a margin expansion?
- The FX rate used (01 Oct 2025) is a snapshot. Which rate methodology was actually applied — spot rate, average rate over a period, hedged rate? ITUK's challenge from James Marsh almost certainly targets this specific point. If ISZE used a rate that was more favourable to ISZE than an annual average would have been, the "mechanical" claim is vulnerable.
- **ISZE's own procurement hedging position is invisible in this analysis.** If IML Japan prices to ISZE in JPY but ISZE hedges FX exposure, then the cost increase to ISZE may be partially or fully absorbed by the hedge — meaning the pass-through to ITUK is not a clean cost recovery but a partial margin transfer to ISZE.

### What is missing
- The actual cost impact to ISZE in EUR terms, line by line, before and after the FX movement.
- Documentation of ISZE's transfer pricing methodology and whether it has been applied consistently across all distributors in this round.
- Evidence of what rate Israel (UMI/UTI, ZPR1 pricing) received. If Israel received a different effective increase to ITUK despite the same JPY exposure, the "mechanical" argument collapses.
- IML Japan's own view on the transfer price methodology. ISZE treats this as settled; IML Japan may not.

### Recommendation
ISZE cannot win this argument by assertion. It needs a transparent cost bridge: JPY procurement cost in FY2025 → EUR equivalent at hedge-adjusted rate → EUR price list impact → ISZE margin before and after. If that bridge shows any margin expansion, the "cost pass-through" framing is factually incorrect and will not survive IML scrutiny (see Logic 6). James Marsh only needs to find one PN where the maths don't hold to destabilise the entire methodology claim.

## LOGIC 2 — FLAT ITUK BILLING AS EXECUTION FAILURE

### What holds up
- It is true that flat parts revenue despite a growing fleet is anomalous and warrants explanation.
- The DVLA car park data (22,389 vehicles, replacing the Hasegawa model at 17,135) is a more reliable baseline than an internal model.
- The logical structure — flat revenue + growing fleet = low capture rate — is arithmetically sound.

### What is uncertain
- **The causal direction is assumed, not proven.** ISZE concludes flat billing = ITUK execution failure. An equally valid reading is flat billing = price-induced demand suppression. If ISZE's prices have increased materially over 3 years while Texaco Delo and aftermarket alternatives held price, the rational dealer response is substitution. Flat billing is consistent with both explanations.
- **The 3-year flat performance period needs to be indexed against ISZE's own price increases over the same period.** If ISZE raised prices in 2023, 2024, and 2025, and ITUK's purchasing flatlined from 2023 onward, the correlation between price increases and stagnation is direct and needs to be explicitly refuted, not ignored.
- The True Sales metric (£580.65/vehicle/year) is presented as a benchmark, but its derivation is unclear. Is this based on actual ITUK throughput, or a modelled expectation? If modelled, against what car park size and service rate assumption?

### What is missing
- A time-series of ISZE's EUR price list changes over 2022–2025, cross-referenced against ITUK's billing trend.
- Dealer-level purchasing data. Is flat billing system-wide across all ITUK dealers, or are some dealers growing while others decline? If the latter, execution failure is plausible. If uniform decline, price or market is more likely.
- Any data on what aftermarket parts ITUK dealers are actually buying and from whom. The "execution failure" conclusion requires evidence that the volume exists but is going elsewhere. Currently this is asserted, not demonstrated.
- ITUK's own explanation. This document attributes motive and failure to ITUK without quoting a single piece of ITUK data or argument in their own words.

### Recommendation
ISZE is making a strong causal claim (execution failure) based on a correlation (flat billing) without ruling out the alternative explanation (price elasticity). This is analytically unacceptable as a final position. The two hypotheses have different policy implications: if execution failure, hold prices and invest in channel development; if price suppression, a targeted price adjustment on high-substitution PNs would recover volume. ISZE has not done the work to distinguish between them.

## LOGIC 3 — PMS GAP AS STRUCTURAL

### What holds up
- The oil filter capture rate (very low — actual vs expected) is a real and dramatic gap. Even with generous error bars on the expected figure, the gap is not a rounding error.
- The three explanations offered (aftermarket substitution, vehicles not being serviced, intermediary margin absorption) are the correct hypothesis set.
- The conclusion that blanket price reduction would not automatically close the gap is logically valid — if vehicles aren't being serviced at all, lower ISZE prices solve nothing.

### What is uncertain
- **The expected volume calculation ( the expected volume filters) is model-dependent and fragile.** It presumably assumes: (a) all 22,389 vehicles are active, (b) each receives at least one oil change per year at a franchised ITUK dealer, (c) the dealer uses genuine Isuzu filters. Each assumption is questionable. A significant portion of the fleet may be serviced independently, beyond warranty, or with annual mileage too low to trigger more than one service. The gap between 100 and the expected volume is so large that even correcting the assumptions still leaves a massive gap — but the very low capture rate should not be presented without disclosing the model sensitivity.
- **The document does not rank the three hypotheses by probability.** Aftermarket substitution, non-service, and intermediary absorption have very different strategic responses. ISZE states that pricing is not the root cause "in all three cases" — but this is only clearly true for cases (b) and (c). For case (a), pricing is precisely the root cause, and ISZE's own Logic 7 (differentiated pricing) acknowledges this.
- No scrap-rate logic has been applied to the 22,389 vehicle figure. If a material portion of that fleet is out of service or scrapped, the expected filter demand is overstated.

### What is missing
- Warranty claim data. IML Japan and ITUK jointly manage warranty. Warranty claims require genuine parts. If warranty claim volumes are also low relative to fleet size, that supports the "vehicles not being serviced" hypothesis. If warranty claims are normal but parts purchases are low, that points toward aftermarket or channel issues.
- Third-party service data. UK MOT and fleet management databases could give an indication of actual service frequency for this vehicle type. This analysis is not referenced.
- Filter purchase data from known aftermarket suppliers for N-series equivalents. If Filtration Group, Mann+Hummel or similar have published volumes for Isuzu-compatible filters, that would directly test hypothesis (a).

### Recommendation
The PMS gap analysis is the most important commercial intelligence ISZE has produced. It is being used primarily as a defensive argument in the pricing discussion rather than being pursued to a definitive diagnosis. ISZE should commit to closing the diagnostic gap before the next pricing round. Presenting a very high non-capture rate to IML Japan as evidence that ITUK has an execution problem, without having investigated which of three hypotheses is actually driving it, will not be credible.

## LOGIC 4 — THE GOLDEN WINDOW

### What holds up
- The ZEV mandate trajectory (24% in 2026, 100% by 2035 for D-Max as an N1 LCV) is real policy, not speculation.
- Fleet replacement lag is a genuine phenomenon. ICE vehicles sold in 2024–2026 will be in service into the 2030s.
- The general logic — peak parts demand for aging ICE fleet before structural decline — is economically coherent.

### What is uncertain
- **"2025–2032" is presented as a defined window, but the underlying modelling is not shown.** Peak parts demand depends on: current fleet age distribution, annual registration rates going forward, service intensity by age bracket, and the rate at which operators switch to EV ahead of mandate deadlines. None of these parameters are quantified here.
- **The D-Max ZEV mandate applies to new registrations. The existing ICE fleet is unaffected by mandate-driven retirement pressure.** But operator behaviour may change faster than mandate logic suggests — if EV total cost of ownership improves sharply, commercial operators may scrap ICE vehicles earlier than normal replacement cycles. The Golden Window could be shorter than 2032.
- The N-series trucks (N35/N75/N50/N62) are not subject to the same ZEV mandate as the D-Max (N1 LCV). The car park data shows N-series at approximately 21,167 vehicles vs a much smaller D-Max population implied. The Golden Window logic is asymmetric across vehicle types and this is not addressed.
- **UK economic conditions affecting fleet operator profitability** (road haulage sector stress, fuel costs, interest rates on asset finance) could compress R&M budgets and accelerate scrappage independently of ZEV transition.

### What is missing
- Age distribution of the current ITUK fleet. If a large proportion of the 22,389 vehicles were registered in 2022–2024, the peak maintenance window is 2027–2032, which is later than implied. If the fleet skews older, some of the window has already passed.
- New registration data for 2024 and 2025. If ITUK vehicle sales have been declining, the fleet is not growing, and the Golden Window logic is weakened.
- A quantified revenue model for the window. "Maximise revenue from the existing fleet" is not a strategy without a number attached.

### Recommendation
The Golden Window is the most compelling piece of ISZE's strategic narrative, but it is being used rhetorically rather than analytically. ISZE should build a fleet-age model using the DVLA data as the baseline, apply realistic service intensity curves by vehicle age, and produce a revenue forecast by year through 2032. That model would reveal whether 2025–2028 or 2028–2032 is the critical period, and would change the urgency calculus on both the pricing discussion and the lubricants launch.

## LOGIC 5 — LUBRICANTS AS ECOSYSTEM STICKINESS

### What holds up
- The behavioural hypothesis (genuine lubricant buyer → genuine filter buyer) is plausible and has analogues in other sectors.
- The data intelligence argument is the strongest element: lubricant purchases at dealer level would provide the first reliable signal of actual service activity, which is the missing variable in the PMS gap analysis.
- Texaco Delo at £309K (ITUK dealer level) is a real and quantified competitive position to displace.

### What is uncertain
- **The behavioural stickiness logic has not been tested in this market.** It is an assumption borrowed from general consumer behaviour and applied to B2B commercial vehicle dealers. A franchised truck dealer's purchasing behaviour may not follow the same logic — procurement managers optimise by PN, not by brand ecosystem loyalty.
- **BESCO is described as a genuine brand from IML Japan but its competitive positioning vs Texaco Delo is unquantified.** Price differential, specification equivalence, availability, and dealer margin are all unstated. If BESCO is more expensive than Texaco Delo and offers comparable specification, dealer incentive to switch is low regardless of ISZE's strategic intent.
- The £309K Texaco Delo figure is described as "ITUK dealer level" — meaning it is the value of Texaco product in the dealer channel, not necessarily all displac­eable by BESCO. Some of that volume may be for non-Isuzu vehicles in mixed-fleet dealers.
- **Launch timing is stated to matter more than margin, but no launch timeline is specified.** If the Golden Window is 2025–2032, and BESCO is not yet launched, the urgency claim is not matched by execution readiness.

### What is missing
- BESCO product specification sheet compared to Texaco Delo specifications required for N-series and D-Max.
- BESCO pricing to ITUK and implied dealer margin vs Texaco Delo dealer margin.
- Any pilot data from other ISZE distributors who may already carry BESCO (Ireland, Belgium, Israel).
- A realistic dealer adoption model: how many of ITUK's dealer network would switch, under what conditions, over what timeframe?

### Recommendation
The lubricants stickiness logic is the weakest of the seven because it rests on an untested behavioural assumption and has no quantified business case attached. If ISZE presents this to IML Japan as a strategic initiative, it needs a launch plan with milestones, a dealer adoption target, a revenue forecast, and a competitive displacement model. As currently framed, it is a hypothesis, not a strategy.

## LOGIC 6 — THE IML TRIANGLE

### What holds up
- The structural observation is correct: ITUK has a direct relationship with IML Japan on vehicles and warranty, giving it an escalation path that bypasses ISZE entirely.
- The requirement that ISZE's position survive IML scrutiny rather than just bilateral negotiation is strategically sound.
- Consistent methodology and transparent FX logic are the right defensive criteria.

### What is uncertain
- **ISZE's assumption that a "credible growth narrative for ITUK" will satisfy IML is untested.** IML Japan's primary concern is vehicle sales in Europe. If ISZE's pricing approach is suppressing dealer network viability in the UK — ISZE's largest market — IML may side with ITUK, not because ITUK is right on the methodology, but because an unhealthy UK dealer network is a bigger problem than a pricing precedent.
- The document assumes ITUK "cannot succeed" if it escalates to IML, but this is stated without analysis of IML's actual interests. IML Japan may have already received signals from ITUK about pricing pressure. ISZE may be the last to know.
- **ISZE's relationship with IML Japan, its own parent, is not characterised.** Is ISZE operationally autonomous? Does IML Japan audit ISZE's transfer prices? Has IML Japan previously intervened in distributor pricing discussions? These institutional dynamics determine how much risk ISZE is actually carrying in Logic 6.

### What is missing
- IML Japan's stated or implied position on distributor pricing discussions. Any prior precedents.
- ISZE's own financial reporting to IML — specifically whether ISZE's GP improvement over 2024 (42.9%) would be visible to IML as margin extraction rather than cost recovery.
- Whether other IML distributors in other regions have faced similar pricing discussions and how those were resolved.

### Recommendation
Logic 6 is the most politically consequential logic and the least analytically developed. ISZE is essentially saying "we can defend this to Japan" without demonstrating it has actually thought through Japan's interests and decision criteria. A pre-emptive briefing to the relevant IML department — before ITUK escalates — is strategically superior to waiting for an escalation and then defending reactively.
