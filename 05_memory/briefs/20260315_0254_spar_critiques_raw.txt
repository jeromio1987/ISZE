## SECTION 2 — WHAT IS UNCERTAIN

### 2.1 Logic 1 — The FX Pass-Through Is Not Self-Evidently Complete

**The circularity problem:** ISZE asserts the increase is a cost pass-through, not a margin grab. But ISZE has not published its actual gross margin *on ITUK specifically* before and after the increase. The €298,911 basket impact tells us what ISZE is charging more. It does not tell us what ISZE's procurement cost actually increased by. If IML Japan provides volume rebates, if ISZE's procurement mix shifted, or if EUR-denominated sourcing offsets some JPY exposure, the pass-through may be *larger than the actual cost increase.* ISZE has not demonstrated cost-in equals price-out.

**The selective rate problem:** The FX rate used is 01 Oct 2025. Why that date? If ISZE uses a spot rate, an average rate, or a rate that happened to be JPY-strong, the methodology is manipulable without being dishonest. ITUK's challenge on "methodology" may be specifically about *which rate, on which date, applied to which basket.* This is not answered in ISZE's framing.

**The asymmetry problem:** Has ISZE ever reduced prices when JPY weakened against EUR? If the mechanism only flows one way (increases when JPY strengthens, no reductions when it weakens), then "cost pass-through" is a description of a ratchet, not a neutral mechanism. A well-resourced ITUK counterargument would pull historical price lists and ask this question directly.

**The 10 Class 0 PNs:** All Source J, zero pricing. This is unresolved. If these are parts ITUK needs and cannot source, they represent a real supply gap that ISZE has not addressed. Alternatively, if they are being handled outside the price list, it creates an audit exposure.

### 2.2 Logic 2 — Flat Billing Attribution Is Genuinely Ambiguous

This is where ISZE's logic is most self-serving and least defensible.

**The car park data problem:** ISZE has just *replaced* its own internal model (Hasegawa, 17,135 vehicles) with DVLA actual data (22,389 vehicles) — a 31% upward revision. This means ISZE's previous True Sales per vehicle calculations, targets, and gap analyses were all based on an undercount. ISZE cannot simultaneously argue (a) the new car park data proves the opportunity is larger than thought, and (b) ITUK's flat billing is entirely ITUK's execution failure. If the car park was undercounted, some of the "gap" was always a model error, not a distribution failure.

**The pricing sensitivity question ISZE refuses to answer:** ISZE asserts flatly that "pricing is not the root cause" of flat billing. This may be true. But ISZE offers no evidence that it is true. The claim rests on the fact that flat performance "predates the 2026 increase." However:
- Prices have been rising in prior rounds too
- GBP/EUR has been unfavourable for ITUK
- Aftermarket parts have become more accessible and cheaper over the same period

The correct test would be: *is the flat performance correlated with cumulative price increases across all rounds, not just 2026?* That analysis does not appear to exist.

**The intermediary margin absorption hypothesis (Logic 3c):** ISZE identifies this as a possibility but does not pursue it. If ITUK's R&M contracts with fleet operators include parts at fixed prices, and ITUK is absorbing the ISZE price increase rather than passing it through, then ISZE's prices *are* causing the flat billing — just with a one-period lag. This is not a remote scenario. It is standard in fleet maintenance contracts.

### 2.3 Logic 3 — The PMS Gap Root Cause Is Unresolved

ISZE presents three explanations: aftermarket substitution, non-servicing, intermediary absorption. Then concludes "pricing is not the root cause" in all three cases.

**This conclusion does not follow from the three explanations:**

- In case (a) — aftermarket substitution — price *is* a direct contributing factor. Aftermarket filters are cheaper. If ISZE's oil filter price is 40% above the Mahle or Fleetguard equivalent, dealers will defect. ISZE acknowledges this as a "genuine competitiveness problem" requiring "targeted analysis" but has not done that analysis. The filter capture rate of very low is not consistent with "some aftermarket leakage." It is consistent with systematic, near-total aftermarket substitution.

- In case (b) — non-servicing — this is the most concerning scenario from a brand and warranty perspective but ISZE treats it as an ITUK problem. If Isuzu vehicles in the UK are not being maintained, that is an IML and ITUK problem, but it is not cost-free for ISZE.

- In case (c) — intermediary absorption — price *is* the cause, as argued above.

**The very low capture rate deserves more weight.** ISZE uses it as a headline number to demonstrate the PMS gap is large. But a very low rate means that for every 1,000 oil change events that should be happening, ISZE is capturing 3. That is not a distribution execution problem. That is near-complete market absence. The explanation that ITUK dealers are buying filters elsewhere is almost certainly the dominant factor at that scale, and price is almost certainly a component of why.

### 2.4 Logic 4 — The Golden Window Has Internal Contradictions

**The EV transition timing is uncertain in both directions:**

*Too optimistic (window closes faster):* The UK ZEV mandate is regulatory, but enforcement, consumer uptake, and fleet adoption are not guaranteed to track the mandate linearly. However, if D-Max EV adoption accelerates faster than the 7–10 year fleet cycle (e.g., through early fleet replacement driven by tax incentives or operator economics), the ICE parts window closes earlier than 2032.

*Too pessimistic (window may not be as valuable as assumed):* An ageing ICE fleet that is being maintained by aftermarket suppliers — which is what the very low filter capture rate implies — does not generate ISZE revenue even if the vehicles are being actively maintained. The Golden Window only generates ISZE revenue if the distribution channel is working. Right now there is no evidence it is.

**The D-Max N1 classification matters:** The ZEV mandate at 24% in 2026 applies to N1 LCVs including D-Max. This means *new* D-Max registrations are constrained, which compresses the future ICE car park growth. ISZE's Golden Window argument assumes the existing fleet ages into peak spend. But if new ICE D-Max registrations slow from 2026, the car park peaks and then begins declining faster than a pure 7–10 year retirement cycle would suggest.

### 2.5 Logic 5 — Lubricants Stickiness Is Behavioural Theory, Not Evidence

**The stickiness assumption is plausible but unvalidated.** The claim that a dealer buying genuine Isuzu oil is "behaviourally more likely" to buy genuine Isuzu filters is a reasonable hypothesis. It has some support in aftermarket loyalty research generally. But:

- ISZE has no evidence from its own network that this correlation holds
- The causal direction is unclear — dealers who are already loyal buy both; lubricants may not *cause* filter loyalty, they may simply *co-occur* with it
- Texaco Delo is entrenched at £309K at dealer level; displacing it requires not just product availability but price competitiveness and dealer incentive — none of which is specified

**Launch timing and execution are undefined.** ISZE says "launch timing matters more than margin." But there is no launch plan, no dealer onboarding mechanism, no pricing structure relative to Texaco Delo, and no specification of which distributors (ITUK only? Belgium? Israel?) are in scope. The urgency claim is not backed by a delivery mechanism.

**The data intelligence argument cuts both ways.** If lubricant purchases reveal which dealers are actively servicing vehicles, they will also reveal which dealers are *not* — and that data may be unwelcome. More importantly, ISZE currently lacks this data. Any strategy dependent on lubricant purchase data as a PMS intelligence layer cannot function until adoption reaches meaningful scale, which may take years.

ISZE needs a time series: ITUK annual purchases (CY2020–CY2024) plotted against ISZE list price index changes. If there is a negative correlation — billing declines as prices rise — that is evidence that pricing is a contributing factor to flat performance. This analysis is absent.

ISZE knows total ITUK billing. It does not appear to have visibility into which dealers are buying what. Without this, the "dealer network execution failure" hypothesis is unfalsifiable. For the specific high-substitution-risk parts (oil filters, EGR valves), ISZE does not appear to have documented the price differential between ISZE genuine prices and the leading aftermarket alternatives available in the UK. ISZE argues ITUK is pushing back for commercial reasons. That may be true.
