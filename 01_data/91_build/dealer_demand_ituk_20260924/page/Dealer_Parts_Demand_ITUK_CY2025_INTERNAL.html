Dealer parts demand — UK truck book

Internal draft · CY2025 · 64 dealers · 22,389 trucks in service · built 24 Sep 2026

The short version. We hold the trucks in service for the market, not for each dealer. So each dealer is given a share of the 22,389 trucks using their share of oil filters, and the model then expects the same share of every other service family from them. The test of that idea is whether it beats simply knowing how big the dealer is — and on this book it mostly does not. That matters, because every euro below sits on top of it.

1. Does the filter share actually hold?

Two questions, answered with numbers rather than asserted.

Is the share stable across families?

Take each dealer's share of network oil filters and compare it with their actual share of another family. If the idea works, those two should agree. On the strongest independent family — fuel filter — the rank agreement is 0.98 and the average dealer is off by 0.22 percentage points. Dealer shares are also narrow: the average dealer holds about 1.6% of the network and the largest holds about 6.3%, so an error of a fraction of a point is a small number of trucks but a large slice of a dealer's own expectation.

Does it beat just knowing the dealer's size?

This is the test that matters. A dealer's total parts volume already tells you a lot about how much of anything they buy. Before the numbers, the shape: each point below is one dealer, their share of oil filters across the bottom against their share of fuel filters up the side. On a working proxy the points sit on the dashed line.

The honest verdict. The filter share wins on 2 of 8 families judged, ties on 0 and loses on 6. On air filter it is actually behind dealer size. The method is not useless — it carries the family split, which size alone cannot, because a big dealer's mix between filters and brakes is exactly what we are short of — but as a better estimate of a dealer's trucks it is not established by this book. Treat every euro below as an allocation on a stated rule, not as a measured gap.

4. What this model can see at all

The method covers parts replaced on a rhythm. It says nothing about repair, body, electrical or hardware demand, where a dealer who sold nothing may simply have had no accident to repair. 6. Limits, stated plainly

  1. One market, one year. The UK truck book is the only place we can see both the trucks and what each dealer bought. Nothing here shows the rule travels to another market.
  2. The fleet is apportioned, not counted. 22,389 trucks is a market figure. Which dealer serves which truck is an assumption on a stated rule.
  3. The rule is not proven to be better than dealer size. See section 1.
  4. Three families carry the biggest gaps and rest on the weakest footing. Brake shoe shows a €874,304 gap on 840 pieces. Clutch booster shows €406,092 on two pieces. Those are questions, not findings.
  5. Currency, warranty and returns are not in this. The file is quantities; the value is a price proxy; nothing here nets off credits or warranty claims.
  6. Four dealers are absent from the expected side. They bought parts but no oil filters, so no share exists and nothing is expected of them. That is a finding about the dealer, and it is labelled as one rather than printed as a zero gap.

Israel is not in this model at all. No Israel row is read. Separately, and not acted on: the dealer survey file used elsewhere in this workspace carries 78 rows labelled Israel LCV, while we hold no dealer names for Israel. That file's labelling is wrong and has been recorded, not corrected.
