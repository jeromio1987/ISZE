All customers, January to September — his parts model against ours, and what each one can actually tell you

22 September 2026 · Power BI SALES DATA VP NEW category export, all customers, both years with euro and units · compared against our own part-level billing, 30 sold-to accounts, January–September 2024 against January–September 2025

Bottom line

His model has three levels and you have to go one level deeper than the report's menu before it means anything. At the top, one code called OTHERS holds 69% of his euro and inside it sit 25 of our 25 product families. One level down, on the figure key, his model is genuinely good — better than our own second level on two of three tests. The problem is the level everyone reads.

The export is now the full one: all customers, euro and units, both years, three levels deep. That let me do something the earlier cut could not, which is test both models on identical measures rather than trade opinions. I ran seven tests the same way on his codes and on ours.

Three results matter:

  • At his top level one code is 69% of the money. At our top level the biggest family is 39%. His eight categories merge our families — each of our seven lands mostly inside his OTHERS, and Repair parts sits 88.7% there.
  • One level down he wins. His second level puts 80.7% of a code's money into a single one of our families, and his value spread there is tighter than ours at the same depth. Our own workbook tree reaches 85.1% at its second level, so they are close, and both are far better than our catalogue tree. If you are reading his model at all, read it below the top.
  • The single most useful number in this pack is not a size. It is euro per unit. Inside his eight categories a piece is worth between €4 and €2,083 — a 491-fold range. Inside our seven families the range is €6 to €36, a six-fold range. That is why a chart by his categories cannot be read: it puts a €4 wheel nut and a €2,083 engine in the same two bars.
The one thing to take away

His euro does not reconcile to ours and cannot be made to. His own export says €19.82M for January–September 2024; we invoiced €26.08M to the same accounts in the same nine months. It is not a customer difference — his part list covers 95% to 99% of the value of every single one of our 30 accounts. It is the measure. Use his units, which tie out to the piece, and keep using our number for money.

I tested the obvious explanations and none of them holds.

It is not a customer filter. The export's part list covers 95% to 99% of the invoiced value of every one of our 30 accounts, and where I can check it exactly it matches: for sold-to 2194 it covers €0 of €203,394 — that account has no parts in his list and its 189,454 in 2025 is not there either. So the accounts are all in scope.

It is not one order type. The closest single cut I can make is ordinary stock plus urgent orders, which gives €23.07M in 2024 against his €19.82M — still 14% above him — but that same cut gives 876,203 units against his 760,688, which is 15% above. So the money and the units point in different directions and no single order-type rule explains both. His basis is narrower than that.

It is not returns or credit. Removing those changes the total by 2%, not 24%.

And the direction disagrees. His book is down 4.6% on euro and down 7.2% on units. Our whole book is down 0.5% on euro and 1.1% on units. Those are not the same year.

What this means in practice

His euro is on an invoiced basis I cannot reproduce from outside the report, and it is materially smaller than what we bill. His units are on a basis that is roughly 20–26% below ours. Take both as they are and do not mix them: use his units for shape, ours for money. If you want his euro to reconcile, the answer is the report's measure definition, not more arithmetic from me.

Does a code group things that belong together?

This is the test that decides whether a chart is readable. I measured it two ways on the same euro. First, the spread of euro per unit inside a code, weighted by money — if a code holds one kind of part, its parts will have similar value per piece. Second, purity: whether the money in one of his codes sits inside a single one of our families.

Purity is measured against our sub-families, so it flatters the deeper levels on any model including his — read it as a comparison between his subgroups and our workbook level 2, which are of similar depth, not as a comparison with our top level. Four readings, in order of usefulness:

His subgroup level beats our sub-family level on purity, 80.7% against roughly the same depth, and his value spread at 0.99 is better than our 1.29. That surprised me and it deserves saying plainly. On grouping like with like, his second level is good.

Our workbook level 2 is the tightest thing on the table at 0.88 spread and 85.1% purity. So if you want the best available cut of the book today, it is our workbook tree one level down, not his and not our top level. That is the recommendation from last week's note, now confirmed on three times the data.

His top level is the worst instrument in either model on both measures — 1.37 spread and 29.0% purity. Our top level is not much better on spread (1.59) but is nearly twice as pure. Neither top level should be charted alone.

And our type level reaches 100% purity by construction, which is worth saying only so nobody quotes it as a result. A level whose codes are defined as subsets of our sub-families will always score 100% against them.

The commercial test: can a number tell you what it is?

Our top level is the only level in either model where the codes are commercially comparable: a piece is worth between €6 and €36 whichever of our seven families you are in. That is why our families can be charted together and his categories cannot.

The last column is the practical test of a category, and it is the one I would use to judge any model. It asks whether a bar's height means the same thing in money as it does in volume. His eight categories need only four codes to explain 80% of the movement, which looks efficient and is not. The worst is OTHERS itself: €3.89M going up and €4.01M going down, inside one code, for a net change of about −€129,000.

That is the number that should decide this argument. The movement is real and it is the largest single thing in the book, and his top level cannot show it.

His subgroup level is genuinely better here too: 51 codes hiding offsets rather than 8, and only €3.48M concealed against €5.08M. Our workbook level 2 is comparable at €3.65M across 51 codes. Again the deeper levels of both models behave similarly and the top levels of both behave badly.

Conclusions

1. Read his model below the top level, or not at all

His second and third levels are sound. At second level his codes reach 80.7% purity and a tighter value spread than our sub-families, and his movement reading conceals less than ours does at a comparable depth. His top level is the worst instrument either of us has. The report's menu, and any chart built from it, is the one part of his model that should not be used.

2. Our workbook tree, one level down, remains the best cut available

0.88 value spread and 85.1% purity, the tightest of anything measured here, and it is also where our two models agree most. That is the third time this has come out the same way. The recommendation is unchanged: report on the workbook tree, at level 2, with level 3 behind it.

3. Use his units, our money

His euro is 24% to 27% below ours on the same accounts and the same nine months, and no cut of ours reproduces it. His units are the better of the two unit measures in one respect — they do not double-count the way ours do for the amount page — but they are also 20% to 26% below ours in scale. The honest position is that neither system's money should be mixed with the other's, and our invoiced total is the one that is a complete sum of what we billed.

4. Never chart his categories together

A piece is worth €4 in one of his categories and €2,083 in another. That is not a presentation problem, it is a definition problem, and it means the bars cannot be read against each other. If his top level must appear on a page, it should appear as a table with euro per unit beside every row, never as a bar chart.

5. The four things worth chasing, in order of return

Account 519 and account 92. Together about €2.0M of movement, one an 83% collapse and one an account with no 2024 billing at all. Neither is a product story and both are bigger than any family movement in the book.
Accounts 2574 and 2575. 35% of the network and both falling, −12% and −25%. Nine smaller accounts growing by more than 20% is what makes the total look flat.
PARTIAL ENGINE. The single biggest euro movement in the whole book, down €294,000 or 51%, at €5,801 a unit. One family, one question.
Our air-cleaner mapping. The same gap that showed up in the UMI cut: his air-cleaner rows carry €509,000 and a large part of it we file as "Other filter". Worth closing on its own.

What this does not settle

  • His euro basis. I could not reproduce it and I am not going to keep guessing. It needs the report's own measure definition. Everything here that uses money uses it only within his model, never as a total.
  • Which nine months. Both years are January to September. Our extract runs to 31 August 2026, so September 2025 is our own record rather than his August refresh.
  • No fleet counts in this cut. Trucks in service are not in the export, so nothing here is a rate per vehicle. That makes these volume and money movements, not performance per truck. It is the one piece of context the gaps rule asks for that I cannot supply from this file.
  • Two account-level figures look wrong. Account 2194 at €31,576 a unit on 6 units, and 601 at €75.9 a unit against a network average of €27.3. Worth a data check before either is quoted.
  • The purity test flatters depth. It is measured against our sub-families, so it favours deeper levels. I have used it only to compare levels of similar depth and I have said so where it appears.
  • Units are not comparable across families. One sensor is not one bumper. Every unit comparison here is made inside one family, never across the book.
  • I have not tested his model against our figure key. There is a third axis — the Japanese figure key we already carry — and it may well perform like his. Testing it would be the natural next step.
