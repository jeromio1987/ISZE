Can we run the parts business off the Power BI category list?

22 September 2026 · Power BI SALES DATA VP NEW category export against our own parts family tree · 19,417 part numbers · 1,921,252 units in January–September 2024 against 1,899,712 in January–September 2025

Bottom line

No — not as the axis of anything a manager reads. The list is a Japanese catalogue index. Where a family tree would name the product, this one hands you a catalogue code, and just over two thirds of the volume and 69% of the money ends up in a bar called OTHERS that names nothing at all.

It is not a bad list. It is a list built to answer a different question, and it answers that one well enough. The problem is what happens when it is used as if it were a family tree: the filter business has to be assembled from nine separate bars spread across two of the eight headings, the same section name appears under several headings, and the largest families by money — sensors, lamps, steering gear, discs and drums — are all filed under OTHERS in their entirety.

There is one genuine win for the Power BI list and it runs against us, which is covered in full below. It classifies the air cleaner correctly and our own PR00 catalogue tree does not. That is the most useful thing this comparison produced.

The one line that matters

Sold by units, the Power BI list is 68.6% OTHERS. That is not a rounding problem, it is the headline bar, and it cannot be acted on.

What was compared

You exported the category table from SALES HISTORY BY CAT.(qty). It carries 19,417 part numbers under three levels: the eight headings in CATEGORY, 237 section names in ISZJ CLASSIFICATION, and 4,883 figure keys in FIG KEY CLASSIFICATION. The part-level rows add up to exactly 100.00% of every section total, so the file is internally sound and nothing was lost in the export.

I joined every one of those part numbers to our own file on the part number. 19,350 of 19,417 matched — 99.93% of the 2024 units and 99.82% of the 2025 units. The 67 that did not match carry 1,700 units, 0.18% of the total, and they are almost all bolts and studs; the largest is a single body-mounting bolt at 1,600 units.

Two things about the export worth knowing before reading any number below. It counts units only, there is no euro in it. And the filter panel names the category, the month and the year but not the customers, so this is the whole book rather than one market. Both years cover January to September, so the year-on-year comparison is like for like but it is nine months, not twelve.

Six of the twenty — sensors, lamps, steering gear, discs and drums, the DEF system and instruments — are €10.89M of our book with no separate bar anywhere on the list. That is 21.7% of our billing. The filter test

Filters are where the difference stops being academic, because it is the one basket where both systems have a clear view of the same money. To read the filter business off the Power BI list you must find and add nine separate bars, spread across two of the eight headings, using four different names for roughly four products.

What the Power BI list gets right — and where it caught us

The fair conclusion includes this, and it runs against our own files.

The list’s AIR CLEANER section holds 120 part numbers and 33,352 units in 2025. Put those parts through our tree and 33,066 of them land cleanly in Maintenance Service > Air Filter, worth €1.20M. That is correct.

Put the same parts through our PR00 catalogue tree — the one our classification paper is built on — and it files 27,135 of them under Comfort parts > Air conditioning, sending only 1,050 into the air filter family. That is wrong, and it is wrong by about €1.05M.

Our own files disagree with each other

Our file still carries two classifications side by side. Measured on your export, they disagree on 19,325 of 19,350 part numbers — 97% of our billed euro. On this evidence the workbook tree is the correct one and the PR00 catalogue tree is the one that misfiles air cleaners. Until that is settled in the file itself, any pack built on the catalogue tree will keep getting the air filter business wrong, and the management note we wrote citing a €28k air filter figure is quoting a reading that no longer holds — the current figure is €1.20M on this population, plus €728k of blower filters that the list files under OTHERS.

Conclusions

1. Do not use the Power BI category list as the axis of a management pack

Two thirds of the volume and 69% of the money land in OTHERS. It cannot rank our families by money and it cannot answer “how is the filter business doing” without nine bars. That is not a criticism of the list, it is a statement about what a catalogue index can do.

2. Keep it, and put it beside the tree the way we keep the price class

It is Japan’s index and it is free. It is the right unit for the catalogue conversation, for price-band work and as a completeness check, and it demonstrably caught a real error in our own air filter mapping. That is worth having. It simply is not the map.

3. Our tree has two problems of its own, and both are now measurable

First, it carries two classifications that disagree on 97% of the euro — that has to be settled in the file, not worked around in reports. Second, 32,083 filter pieces (16% of the filter basket) are still in an unsplit “Filters” leaf, and another €1.44M sits under “nothing set”. Both are fixable and both currently cap how precise any filter or family figure can be.

4. Report on the workbook tree, and stop stopping at the first level

The workbook tree is the one that reads correctly here. And within it, the second level is where the meaning is: the first level puts €7.97M of emission parts and €4.93M of brakes at similar heights as much smaller families. Read at level 2, with level 3 behind it for investigation.

5. The next thing worth doing is the clean re-cut, not another comparison

What would settle this properly is one table with your three levels and our three levels on the same row, over a full twelve months, with euro on both sides. The rows are built and sitting on disk from this pass, so it is a re-cut rather than a project. Everything above is measured; nothing has been changed.

What this comparison does not settle

  • No euro in your export. Every unit figure is exact and every euro figure is ours, over a different window. The two must not be read as one total.
  • Which customers were selected is not visible. The filter panel names category, month and year only, and the total is the whole book. If you meant to send one or two markets, the numbers will move and I will redo it.
  • Nine months, twice. Both years cover January to September, so the comparison is like for like, but it is not a full year and the 2025 side is a part-year.
  • The measure itself. The export comes from the report’s sales table, which sits above a separate demand table on the same page. I read the sales one. Whether those units are invoices or orders is not something the file alone can tell me, and it changes what the movement means.
  • Pieces are not comparable across families. Adding units only makes sense inside one basket, which is why the nine-bar comparison above is drawn on filters alone. A single total of 949,856 pieces across every family is not a quantity of anything.
  • No fleet counts in this cut. Trucks in service are not in your export, so nothing here is expressed as a rate per vehicle. That makes these volume movements, not performance per truck.
  • 67 part numbers are in your export and not in our file — 1,700 units, 0.18%, mostly bolts and studs, the largest a single body-mounting bolt at 1,600 units.
