The five layers, in the order the data actually flows

Each layer answers one question, consumes the layer above it, and hands its output to the one below. The state chips say what kind of figure that layer really produces, because a layer that looks authoritative but is mostly modelled is the most dangerous object in this estate.

2 · PRISM

What those trucks should consume, and what we actually bill them

The part number is the only key the three layers share

PRISM rolls its service demand up to a part number through the catalogue. AIM classes part numbers directly. Fitment links a part number to a model code, and the model code carries the weight class. Nothing else is common to all three, which is why the part-number registry is the real build and every screen on top of it is a view.

What the part number carries

  • the aftermarket class (L1 / L2 / L3) and its competitive exposure;
  • the service category, oil, fuel, air, urea, brake or clutch, and its expected demand;
  • the catalogue reference that reaches a model group, and through it the fleet;
  • its price level on the list, and whether it has a usable list price at all.

A part number with no documented fitment cannot be joined to a weight class, so it cannot be given a target spend. That is the most common reason a figure on this page is blank rather than small.

Until one is settled, any target spend attached to an NLR part number is a judgement, not a measurement, and is marked as one wherever it appears.

The four joins that make five layers one system

Two of them connect the layers to each other and two connect the data to a market. None of the four is automatic today, and each one has a named weakness that the page states instead of smoothing over.

Join 1 · by part number

Demand, competition and fitment all land on the same key

PRISM counts expected and actual quantity per part number. AIM classes the same part number. Fitment ties that part number to a model, and the model carries the weight class. This is the join that turns a fleet count into a parts decision, and it is why the part-number registry is the real build.

Where it is weak: a part number with no usable list price is out of reach of any price decision, however good. 251 part numbers carried EUR 4.96M of the CY2025 billed book with no list price; a pull in August 2026 closed 220 of them, leaving 31 part numbers and EUR 0.59M.

Join 2 · model code to weight class

Weight class is how a part number reaches the fleet

NPR75 is 7.5 tonnes. FRR90 and FSR90 are 9.0 tonnes. The ranges are named N35, N55, N65, N75 and F110 / F135. The project workbook, however, carries one undifferentiated light-duty bucket, so a 3.5 tonne vehicle can be charged the target spend of a 7.5 tonne vehicle. For Portugal that understates spend per unit by roughly a quarter, so the market total moves while nothing in the market has changed.

Where it is weak: no per-market split by N35 / N50 / N62 / N75 / F110 / F135 exists anywhere on disk. Only recent-arrival shares and claim counts exist, and neither of those is a fleet mix.

Join 3 · by distributor and market

The only join that appears in every layer

Fleet rows, billing, expected demand, aftermarket exposure and dealer scores all key on the distributor. It is not one column: a sold-to code, a payer code and a distributor code all exist, and the Israeli payer covers two separate businesses that split at sold-to level.

Where it is weak: Israel's vehicles carry one distributor name in the warranty file, so the truck book and the pickup book cannot be split from claim data alone. Israel age figures are pooled and are labelled as pooled on this page.

Join 4 · by month, in two directions

What we invoice the distributor, and what the distributor invoices its dealers

These are two separate measurements and must never be drawn as one line. The ratio between them is how much stock the distributor is sitting on: close to parity means the parts flow through to the dealers, low means they stay in the distributor's warehouse.

Where it is weak: sales-to-dealer files exist for six of the eighteen markets. Reading a network ratio without the coverage line beside it would overstate how much of the book is really visible. The ratio never reaches 100 per cent even where coverage is full, because the distributor sells but does not bill every part in the same period.

What is actually known per market — and what is not

Eighteen distributors form the commercial base. The vehicle register covers seven markets and excludes the UK and Israel by design; Italy is out of scope entirely. A chip that says excluded is not a zero: it means that layer has nothing to divide by, and printing a number there would be an invention.

Which gap blocks which layer

Six open source gaps and three closed ones, each with an identifier so it can be tracked in the integrity register. The right-hand column says what this page does instead of printing a number.

IDWhat it isWhich layer it blocksWhat this page shows instead
UIO-FCAST The vehicles-in-service forecast for 2026–2030 removes about 3 per cent of the fleet a year while the 1990–2025 history implies about 10 per cent. It is overstated by 7.8 per cent in 2026, rising to 37.7 per cent in 2030, and far worse by market. Israel is roughly 25 per cent low at the same time, in every year. Car park, and every figure that divides by it: spend per unit, capture, the FY27 target No 2026–2030 denominator appears anywhere on this page. Every figure is CY2025 or earlier, and the forecast half of the workbook is named as under repair rather than quoted.
VIN-IL Israel is absent from the vehicle register, so there is no vehicle age and no model detail for the Israeli book from identity data. Car park, and part-level demand for Israel Israel age is shown as a warranty-claim measurement, pooled across trucks and pickups, and flagged as pooled. No per-model fleet split is drawn for Israel.
VIN-ITUK The UK identity list arrived on 23 July 2026 and supplies 29 935 distinct registrations. It carries no live-fleet flag, no mileage and no transmission, so it is not yet a fleet.
