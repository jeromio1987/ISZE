Part IWhat we are pricing

01What we are actually pricing

Everything that follows in sections 04 and 05 is built on the PMS capture model. That model sees fourteen categories. This is what the book actually looks like, in euros.

Correction to scope

The PMS capture model exists because PMS was the programme that needed it. It was never a statement that PMS is the business. On CY2025 billing, PMS-type categories are €4.10M — 12.7% of a €32.3M book.

Body is larger, at €5.04M. It is not an edge case at the margin of the model; it is the biggest single thing we sell. And it behaves nothing like a wear part — insurance-assessed, pattern-part competed, damage-driven rather than mileage-driven. It needs its own benchmark and its own demand logic, not an extension of the filter model. Israel has already raised price complaints there.

The top sub-groups make the same point: Panels €2.60M, Sensors €1.98M, Lamps €1.32M, Friction €1.30M, Steering gear €1.14M, Discs & drums €1.07M, Filters €1.03M.

Three data defects found while producing this figure

  • cy2025_raw.csv carries an embedded grand-total row. Summing the net value column naively returns €66.0M instead of €32.3M — a 104% overstatement. The row has no sales type, which is the only way to catch it.
  • That embedded total disagrees with its own lines. It reads €33.71M against €32.31M actually present — a €1.4M / 4.3% gap.
  • The part-number columns are transposed. Material holds the part number; Material Number holds the description. Joining on the column named "Material Number" returns zero matches — which is at least loud. A fuzzy join would have failed silently.

02What the formula cannot see

The class factor is a supply-side attribute. It describes what IML calls a part, not what the buyer will pay for it.

FOB_EUR = BP_JPY / FX × source_discount SP_EUR = FOB_EUR / class_factor

The formula is sound and it is not in question here — it is IML-owned and this proposal leaves it untouched. The issue is what sits on top of it. A class factor gives every part in class C a 53% margin whether it costs €2 or €2,000, whether an aftermarket copy exists or not, and whether the buyer is replacing a filter at a planned service or a water pump on a vehicle that is off the road.

Three consequences follow, and all three are visible in our own data:

It is blind to competition. On the categories where we hold a benchmark, our street price ranges from 1.61× to 3.04× the aftermarket. Those are not judgements about brand premium — they are the arithmetic of a factor that was never asked to look outward.

It has no feedback loop. FY2026 was a uniform 5.3%. That price change carried no information: no capture rate, no competitive index, no realised margin fed into which parts moved. A pricing system that cannot learn is an indexation rule.

It is fighting the PMS programme. The Kosha plan takes VP Sales from €32.3M to €35.3M while GP% falls from 36.2% to 31.3%. That is a volume plan. But price is the only lever the class system operates, and it currently suppresses volume on exactly the competed, high-frequency parts where the capture gap lives.

03How everyone else prices

No manufacturer publishes its parts pricing method — Toyota, Ford and Nissan treat it as commercial confidence. What is documented is industry practice, from the consultancies and pricing vendors who implement it.

The method they have moved away from is ours

Cost-plus — a standard markup on landed cost — is described consistently as the legacy approach. Its documented failure mode is worth quoting against our own formula: when an OEM buys a part more cheaply and applies the same markup, it silently becomes cheaper than the competition without knowing it. Our class factor does exactly this, in both directions.

What replaced it is market-based and value-based pricing: the price reflects what the buyer believes the part is worth, informed by continuous competitor intelligence, and set per part rather than per category.

Where OEMs deliberately take margin

Two lifecycle positions come up repeatedly, and both are already in this proposal:

New parts — no third party has tooled for them yet, so there is no competitive ceiling. This is the launch book in section 14, and it is standard practice rather than an aggressive idea.

Parts leaving production — competitors stop manufacturing them because volumes no longer justify it, and scarcity returns pricing power to the OEM. Worth noting because you raised end-of-production parts as a source of large percentage increases. They are not an accounting artefact. Taking margin there is deliberate industry practice. That does not remove the need to separate those lines from ISZE decisions in the distributor conversation, but it does mean the position is defensible.

One further point argues against part of our own launch logic. Aftermarket demand is documented as materially less price-elastic than retail demand, because a part is need-to-have and downtime carries its own cost. The literature is explicit that loss-leader promotions on such parts erode margin on items the customer was going to buy anyway. That is an argument for the split tactic and against the habit tactic applied broadly — buy the habit on genuinely competed wear parts, never on parts the customer has no choice about.

How often they adjust

The uncomfortable arithmetic

A price set once a year is, in a moving market, the right price on the day it is set and drifting on the other 364. Our competitive position is not a number we choose annually — it is a number the market re-decides every week while our list stands still.

This does not argue for repricing ISZE continuously; distributor relationships and SAP make that impractical. It argues for separating the annual list revision from a quarterly competitive review, which is the cadence this proposal recommends: annual list revision by IML, quarterly competitive review by ISZE.

Part IIWhere we stand

04Where we stand against the aftermarket

Genuine street price is list × 1.40 distributor margin × 1.275 dealer margin = ×1.785. Measured against the Eucon competitor average.

Two cautions on this chart, both of which matter for how it is used. Eucon covers 44 part numbers network-wide, so a category median rests on three observations and the spread inside a category exceeds 2× — one fuel filter reads 2.46×, another reads 1.20×. And it will appear to contradict the competitiveness dashboard, which shows D-Max PMS parts at 0.66–0.77×. Both are correct: that dashboard measures D-Max at authorised-service-centre level, this measures the network against independent aftermarket. They are different comparisons.

We are benchmarking against the wrong competitor

Everything above measures us against the independent aftermarket. That tells us where we lose a transaction. It does not tell us where we lose the vehicle — and with it the whole parts annuity behind it.

A fleet buyer choosing between an N75 and a Canter compares running cost including parts. A tender scores a parts basket. In both cases the reference is another manufacturer's genuine part, not a pattern part. The aftermarket is the floor. The competitor OEM is the benchmark. We measure only the floor.

This is why the premium target in this engine is not a flat rate, and why it currently does nothing at all. Every category carries a value and a basis, and a category whose basis is unbenchmarked does not move:

policy_premium: by_category: brake_pad: {value: 1.40, basis: placeholder} require_basis: [oem_benchmark, iam_derived]

Run the allocation today and δposition fires on nothing — n_unbenchmarked_premium: 213. Not one line moves because of where it sits against the market. That is the correct output, not a defect: a premium nobody has measured is a guess with a decimal point, and the engine must not launder it into a target. What still differentiates the book is parc momentum, covered in section 09 — so the result is a smarter uniform increase, not a repricing.

A second unknown, and it is testable

We do not know whether IML has already embedded a premium in BP_JPY, or whether it varies by part type. Layering a flat ISZE premium on top of an unknown IML one compounds two errors.

This can be checked: for parts where we hold both BP_JPY and an external benchmark, examine whether the implied street premium varies systematically by part type in a way the class factor does not explain. If BP_JPY is clean cost, the premium should be broadly flat within a class. If IML has already loaded certain categories, it will not be. That test should run before we set any target.

One route is closed, and one is open now

Closed: bridging equivalence through aftermarket cross-references. The hope was that a single Febi or Bosch part listing both an Isuzu OE number and a competitor OE number would give us the match for free. Checked against a real part — oil filter 8982705240 — and the cross-reference lists twelve aftermarket brands and no competitor OE numbers at all. That is not an omission: a filter for a 4JJ1 does not fit a Fuso 4M42. Competing OEMs' parts are genuinely not interchangeable, so no free equivalence bridge exists.

Open, and arguably better: compare the job, not the part. Capped-price service plans are published, cover the whole basket, and sidestep equivalence entirely — you are matching a service interval, not a part number. They are also precisely what a fleet buyer and a tender actually score.

This changes where the benchmark programme should start

Not with part prices. Start with published service-plan pricing — it is free, comparable, needs no licence, and it is the number that decides a vehicle purchase. The parts annuity follows the vehicle.

It also reframes the D-Max interval change. Going from 20,000 to 15,000 km raises our parts revenue per vehicle by a third — and simultaneously worsens our cost-per-kilometre position against exactly these competitors. The same fact is an opportunity on one measure and an exposure on the other, and only one of the two is currently being managed.

The genuine difficulty is equivalence, not price collection. An Isuzu oil filter cannot be compared to a Mercedes oil filter by name — it has to be "the filter fitted to a 7.5-tonne Canter with a 3.0-litre engine" against "the filter fitted to an N75 with a 3.0-litre engine." That is an application-level match, which is what step 3 buys and what steps 1 and 2 have to do by hand.

Blind spot — body parts

  • Body is not among the fourteen PMS categories and does not appear in the action map at all. The engine has never seen it.
  • It is also where Israel has raised actual price complaints.
  • And it needs a third benchmark again — body competes with pattern parts and insurance assessors, not with OEM genuine or with mainstream aftermarket.
  • Marked excluded_blind_spot in the engine so nothing prices it until the category is properly covered.

Part IIIThe method

06What the old way would have given

Judging a new method on its own is not judging it. The engine has to be measured against the alternative — and the alternative turns out not to be one thing.

"Cost-plus" describes two different procedures that currently point in opposite directions:

MethodWhat it doesWho chooses the increase
Strict cost-plusRe-derive SP = FOB / class factor each year and accept the resultCost and FX. Nobody chooses
Target markupApply a chosen percentage to last year's priceWe do. It is a margin decision

The difference is not academic. Holding IML base price constant, FX moving from 146.6 to 163.7 JPY/EUR makes the euro cost of a part fall. Strict cost-plus would therefore have cut prices by about 10.4% over that period. The applied increase was +5.3%.

Both can be true — IML base prices may have risen enough to cover the gap. The point is that nobody currently knows the split, and a distributor asking "why has this gone up when the yen moved in our favour?" is asking a fair question we cannot presently answer per part.

The price bridge

Any price movement decomposes exactly into five terms:

prior price
  + IML base price effect
  + FX effect
  + source mix effect  (Japan vs Thailand)
  + class factor effect
  + ISZE decision  (the residual — the only part we chose)
= new price

It reconciles by construction, so the terms sum to the headline percentage rather than approximately to it. That residual is the number worth being able to state. Everything above it is pass-through of decisions taken elsewhere, and saying so precisely is a stronger position than absorbing the whole increase as though it were ours.

The module also runs turnover and margin under each policy — frozen, strict cost-plus, target markup, engine. One deliberate limitation: volume is held constant. With no measured elasticity, any volume response would decide the comparison by assumption rather than by evidence. It becomes a genuine backtest once sell-out history accumulates — the same function, with a real volume term.

What this needs before it produces real numbers

  • BP_JPY history per part, one snapshot per revision
  • Dated PR00 snapshots — the same blocker as the realisation waterfall
  • FX and class factors by year, which we already hold

07Two planes — and why one of them is bent

Everything so far runs on what ISZE invoices to distributors. That measures distributor stocking behaviour, not end-market demand. Sell-out data from January 2025 adds the second plane.

This is usually described as "more data." It is more than that — it corrects a defect in the current measurement.

The weighted increase is currently self-biasing

The declared increase is a Laspeyres index weighted on the last twelve months of ISZE→DB volume. Announce an increase with notice, and distributors buy ahead of it. That pre-buy inflates recorded volume on precisely the parts that went up — which then overweights those same parts in next year's index.

The measuring instrument is bent by the action it measures. A dealer does not pre-buy on an ISZE price letter, so weighting on sell-out breaks the loop.

Two more things the second plane settles

Pass-through becomes observable. A 3% list increase is currently assumed to reach the market as 3%. It may arrive as 0% on competed lines the distributor absorbs, and as 6% on captive lines where they take the opportunity. Those are opposite problems needing opposite responses, and today we cannot tell them apart.

The street factor stops being a guess. We assume list × 1.40 distributor × 1.275 dealer = ×1.785, flat, across eighteen distributors whose gross margins range from 40.8% to 47.1%. A flat factor across that spread is not a measurement. Sell-out gives the real number per distributor per category.

Distributors set their own resale prices and this changes nothing about that — the factor is an internal estimate used to compare our position against the aftermarket, not a price we expect or recommend anyone to charge.

The design rule

Optimise on the sell-out plane. Constrain on the billing plane.

Demand evidence, competitive position and elasticity come from what dealers actually buy. The negotiated weighted increase stays declared and measured on ISZE→DB, because that is the number a distributor can audit. The bridge between the two planes is itself measurable, with its own diagnostics — pass-through, stocking swing, and channel margin.

Until sell-out lands, every function degrades to the billing plane and records its basis as MISSING rather than quietly substituting.

08How the engine allocates

Every line receives a base rate plus a set of named, signed adjustments, each traceable to a source file. A distributor query on any line resolves to a reason code.

Δ(pn) = Δ_base + δ_position + δ_momentum + δ_lifecycle + δ_market subject to Δ_floor ≤ Δ(pn) ≤ Δ_cap per-line guardrail Σ(w · Δ) = Δ_target w = L12M revenue share

The base is solved jointly with the caps by bisection, not before them. Clamping outliers shifts the weighted index, so solving the two separately lets the declared increase drift off target — the engine lands on 3.0000% exactly.

The fifth row matters more than it looks. Where we are already below market and still holding under 45% of the wallet, price is not the constraint — availability, lead time or dealer behaviour is. Cutting further burns margin on a problem price cannot solve.

None of these gates can fire today. Each requires a benchmarked premium target for the category, and no category has one — so every row currently resolves to hold. The machinery is built and tested; it is waiting on the OEM basket study in section 04, not on further modelling.

What this engine deliberately does not do

  • No elasticity coefficient. Not measurable at ISZE level — we sit too far from the market, and the existing file measures distributor stocking, not end-market response.
  • No realisation waterfall. We hold one PR00 snapshot. Applied backwards it yields 0.8865 → 0.8956 → 0.9185 → 0.9416, which is the 2026 increase read in reverse, not leakage, and must not be presented as such.
  • No discount-versus-SPA decomposition. The billing extract carries net value only — no gross, no condition types.
  • No action on 522 part numbers billed with no PR00 entry: €4.96M, 15.1% of CY2025 revenue. Plus 800 PNs / €1.82M on superseded numbers. Data fix precedes pricing.

Correction to an earlier draft

A previous version of this document said the engine "holds all 213 cells flat." That is no longer true and should not be repeated. Competitive position gates are idle, but parc momentum is wired and moves 60 cells. The book is differentiated — just on demand, not on competitiveness.

Which is a problem, and it has been fixed

Gating position on evidence while leaving momentum ungated produced an indefensible result: the engine would raise brake pads 6.5% because the parc was growing, on a category we can see sits at 2.38× the aftermarket. It declined to act on the evidence it had, and acted on the evidence that says nothing about willingness to pay.

The principle now applied: evidence sufficient to veto is a lower bar than evidence sufficient to act. A street reading too thin to justify cutting a line is still good enough to stop momentum pushing it up. Ten cells — air filter at 2.77×, cabin filter 3.04×, fuel filter 2.46×, brake pads 2.38×, brake rotors 1.61× — now receive the base rate only.

What this means for the honest reading of the engine

  • Today it is a smarter uniform increase, not a repricing. Three momentum buckets spread around a base rate. That is better than one flat number, and a long way short of what it is designed to do.
  • No competitive correction happens at all. Not one line moves because of where it sits against the market, because no category has a benchmarked target.
  • A reason code needs renaming before anything distributor-facing. All 213 cells carry NO_EVIDENCE_HOLD while averaging +3.4%. The label says hold and the behaviour is a rise. Split it into POSITION_HOLD and THETA_MOVE.

10The 3% bind, once position unlocks

The weighted increase is a Laspeyres index on last-twelve-months volume. Correcting our competitive position and delivering 3% turn out to pull in opposite directions.

Recommendation

Either the 3% comes down, or brake correction waits, or benchmark coverage expands so the burden spreads across more lines. These are the only three options and the choice is commercial, not analytical.

Part IVThe two new models

11The assumption that dominates everything

Replacement mileage. It moves the answer by two orders of magnitude more than the choice of pricing tactic.

Across 54 parameter combinations the hierarchy is unambiguous:

Replacement mileage is worth roughly 330 times more than the tactic debate. Every hour spent arguing about launch multipliers before the intervals are calibrated is an hour spent on the fourth-most-important variable.

This can be measured, not assumed

For any existing part number:

implied replacement km = annual km × capture rate ÷ units billed per vehicle-year

All three inputs already exist — ITUK sell-out by part number, UIO for parc, and capture by distributor and category. The capture adjustment is essential: raw billing over parc gives only the replacements we captured, so in the defend zone the implied interval reads far too long. We would conclude brake pads last 200,000 km when the customer simply bought them elsewhere.

Calibrated intervals then transfer directly to carryover architecture on the new models, leaving only the genuinely new parts needing judgement. It doubles as a data-integrity check: an oil filter that comes out at 5,000 km means the capture rate or the parc is wrong.

12D-Max 2.2L — the emissions windfall

New part numbers carry zero weight in the Laspeyres index. The 3% does not bind on them, and there is no old price for a distributor to compare against.

The SCR system is an entirely new set of part families with no predecessor. DEF pump, DEF injector, SCR catalyst, NOx sensors upstream and downstream. No old part number, therefore no old price, no distributor reference point and no aftermarket equivalent. This is the largest block of genuinely free pricing in the FY2027 cycle and it is available once.

Second, the shorter interval delivers 33% more service events per vehicle per year on everything tied to the oil interval — volume growth requiring no commercial action at all. It also means filters are what a distributor touches most often, so filter pricing is the visible basket.

Where to price up, and where not to

Price up on the AdBlue hardware — NOx sensors, DEF pump, DEF injector, SCR catalyst. These are failure-driven, expensive, and nobody holds a reference for them. Do not price up the urea filter: it is a scheduled consumable the dealer sees every service, it is trivial to copy, and it belongs on the visible basket.

The SCR component breakdown is inferred from standard AdBlue architecture rather than read from an Isuzu parts catalogue. Some engine ancillaries may also carry over from RZ4E. Verify against the actual part-number list before quoting.

13P700 — a different windfall entirely

P700 is the new-generation N-Series: 3.5t, 7.5t and 13.5t chassis, the largest UK product overhaul in thirty years. It replaces the core of our parts business.

The engines largely carry over — the new 175 PS 3.0-litre sits between the existing 150 PS 3.0 and 190 PS 5.2. And the predecessor already ran SCR: the 5.2-litre 4HK1E6C uses DPD plus SCR for Euro VI. So the AdBlue windfall does not apply here. Distributors hold reference prices for every one of those parts.

What is genuinely new is the ISIM dual-clutch nine-speed gearbox, the ADAS pack (BSIS, DWS, TSR, TPMS), LED lighting, new cabs, and a 13.5t weight class we have never priced. Which makes the relevant question what the DCT replaces.

Clutch packs should outlast the manual clutch they replace. Wet packs are oil-cooled and shifts are electronically controlled — none of the driver abuse that kills a manual disc at 150–200,000 km on a 7.5-tonne vehicle. Higher value per event, fewer events, no aftermarket equivalent.

Mechatronic life is coupled to fluid discipline. Skipped fluid changes are what kill DCT valve bodies. If the vehicle comes to us for the fluid service, the mechatronic sits at the long end of that fork; if it does not, the short end. That is a commercial link between a €200 service item and a €2,800 assembly — and it argues for pricing the fluid service to be taken, not to make margin.

The fluid interval is knowable rather than assumable: it will be stated in the P700 maintenance schedule, the same source that gave us the D-Max 20,000→15,000 km finding. The three failure-driven intervals stay uncertain until Israel's fifteen months becomes four or five years.

14Launch tactics

Launch price is the one decision here that cannot be revisited. An existing part can rise 3% a year forever; a launch price set too low is never recovered.

Why the freeze works

Holding a line flat while the rest of the book rises makes it progressively cheaper in relative terms — and that arrives at roughly the moment an aftermarket alternative appears.

15Warranty sequencing

A sequencing option that carries an internal cost. It should not be adopted without the agreement of the warranty function.

Two features of the warranty period bear on price. Parts fitted under warranty are still supplied and invoiced by ISZE — an earlier version of this model treated that demand as revenue that never occurred, which understated launch-phase volume substantially. And an aftermarket part cannot be fitted to a warranty repair, so in that channel there is no competitive substitution at any price.

The option: for failure-driven parts only — aftertreatment, sensors, injectors, and not scheduled service items, which the customer pays for regardless — set the launch price toward the upper end of the policy band, then hold it flat once warranty ends rather than applying the annual increase.

The commercial logic is timing, not who pays. Price is set when volume is lowest and no substitute exists, then held while volume grows and the aftermarket arrives.

Costs and cautions, stated plainly

  • The approach raises warranty expense per vehicle during the covered period. That figure is visible to IML and is read partly as a product-quality indicator. It should be quantified and presented alongside the pricing proposal, not left to emerge later.
  • ISZE and the warranty function sit within the same group result. The gain is in competitive position and timing, not in the transfer itself, and the proposal should be made on that basis.
  • It requires a multi-year commitment to hold price. Abandoning the freeze after two years leaves us worse off than never having started.

Part VGovernance and next steps

16The three price lists

PR00

Full engine as described. 15,355 lines.

ZPR1 — Israel

Currently a flat ~10% below PR00, class-dependent. Israel is 17.6% of network revenue at 40.8% GP against a 44.3% network average — the discount is being applied even in categories where it buys nothing. Replace the blanket 10% with a per-category differential derived from the Israeli street data we already scrape. Not for price level: fifteen months sits entirely inside the warranty period and says nothing about the paid channel in years four to seven, and ZPR1 needs restating to a PR00 basis before any read-across.

CIS — Armenia and Kazakhstan

Currently SP = FOB / 0.40, a 60% gross margin applied class-independently. The highest margin in the entire system, applied to the markets most exposed to grey import, with no ability to differentiate at all.

The competitor is not primarily the independent aftermarket. It is Qingling — the Chongqing joint venture, licensed N-series and F-series production with substantially overlapping part numbers. Kazakhstan shares a land border with China, and Kazakhstan and Armenia both sit inside the EAEU customs union, so goods landing in one reach the other with no further customs event. Both our CIS distributors are inside the same customs area as the leak.

Recommendation — no blanket CIS cut

If Qingling parts land at 30–50% of our price, a 15% cut does not win the sale; it surrenders margin without changing behaviour. And a deep cut on a class-independent list is the easiest arbitrage in our system — we would risk funding a grey channel back into Europe.

Instead: make CIS class-dependent — that is the structural fix, and without it no differentiation is possible at all. Cut to 40–45% GM where a Chinese equivalent exists, hold 60% or above on Euro VI/VII aftertreatment and electronics they cannot follow, and put re-export monitoring in place before any cut.

On tracking the flow through customs data: China publishes HS-code aggregates only — no brand, no part number. The commercial bill-of-lading databases carry shipper and consignee detail but are built on maritime manifests, and this lane is rail and road via Khorgos and Dostyk. We will not get part-level visibility. GPS spend-per-unit for our CIS distributors is already the measurement we need, and it is free.

17How this gets audited

A pricing engine is worth what it can be challenged on. Six layers, in the order a reviewer would work through them.

#LayerAnswersStatus

4CoherenceDo the prices make sense next to each other?Not yet running
5ReproduceDoes a logged run re-execute to the same answer?Running

Layer 5 is the one that makes the rest mean anything. Recording a configuration hash proves nothing unless something re-runs from it and compares. It does: re-executing a logged allocation reproduces it to a maximum numeric drift of 1.8 × 10⁻¹², which is floating-point noise from the solver rather than instability.

Current verdict is WARN — no failures, three honest gaps:

Open audit gaps

  • Evidence age is untracked. There is no evidence_asof column, so a benchmark scraped last week cannot be distinguished from one scraped last year. A price moving on stale evidence looks identical to one moving on fresh evidence.
  • Coherence checks have nothing to run against. Three checks are written and idle: price ladders (a larger variant priced below a smaller one), kits priced above the sum of their components, and supersession continuity. The last is live — 800 part numbers and €1.82M are billed on superseded numbers today.
  • Outcome audit needs the second plane. Whether a price change produced the revenue it was meant to cannot be checked from billing alone.

Two governance points that are not code. Separation of duties — whoever sets the policy dials should not be the only person who reviews the output, and the sign-off belongs in the run record. And a challenge log: when a distributor queries a line, record the query and the resolution. Over a couple of cycles that becomes the evidence base for what is actually survivable per line, which is currently a matter of opinion.

20What this changes

The pricing round stops being a single negotiated percentage and becomes an allocation with a reason code on every line. That alone changes the distributor conversation from "3% across the board" to something checkable:

"Your top fifty service lines are flat or down. Your last-twelve-months basket moves +2.6%, below the 3% network figure. Separately, N lines carry IML base-price corrections — listed here with BP_JPY before and after."

Separating IML corrections and end-of-production parts from ISZE decisions is not presentational. Those are the lines that generate the complaints, and the answer to each is completely different. Mixing them into one weighted average is what makes the conversation unwinnable.

And the two new models are where the method can be proven without spending a single basis point of the increase — because parts with no history cannot appear in a comparison. That is the wedge: implement it properly on P700 and D-Max 2.2L, demonstrate it, then extend to the legacy book.
